Inside the Basement

A typical private-well home runs its own water utility from one wall of the basement. This page does two things: shows a homeowner exactly what each piece of equipment is and what it needs, and shows an operator or investor the full range of services — how long each takes, when it takes two technicians, which tools come out of the van, and what it costs.

00 — Start Here

Which system does your home have?

Walk to where the water pipe enters your basement. What you see next to it answers the question in ten seconds.

You're on a private well if you see…
✔  A pressure tank — a waist-height or taller steel tank, usually navy blue or gray, with a small gray switch box and a pressure gauge at its base. This is the single fastest tell: if you have a pressure tank, you have a well.

✔  No water meter and no water bill.

✔  A well cap in the yard — a metal disc about 6" across, sticking a foot out of the ground.

✔  A circuit breaker labeled "well pump."
What it meansYour home is its own water utility. Nobody tests or treats this water unless you arrange it — everything on this page is your infrastructure.
You're on public water if you see…
✔  A water meter where the pipe enters — a round brass body with a dial or digital register, sometimes with a wired remote reader.

✔  A water bill from a town or utility.

✔  A curb-stop cover — a small metal lid in the lawn or street edge.

✔  No pressure tank — street pressure does that job.
What it meansYour utility handles testing and federal compliance to the tap. Hardness, taste, and anything your home's own plumbing adds are still yours — which is why softeners and filters appear in public-water basements too.

~46% of NH homes and ~50% of ME homes are on private wells (NHDES · Maine CDC). Some homes also have a dug or shallow well feeding outdoor spigots alongside town water — if you have both a meter and a pressure tank, you may have both systems.

When one tech is not enough: a second technician is required by weight (media vessels run 150–300 lbs; a submersible pump hangs on hundreds of feet of water-filled pipe), by safety (a pull going wrong at the wellhead needs a second set of hands), by licensing (pump work legally requires a licensed installer on the job), or by trade span (radon aeration is plumbing + venting + electrical in one visit). That is why crew capacity — not demand — sets an operator's revenue ceiling.
